An upgrade that changes its recipient is more interesting than an upgrade that only improves a statistic. *Wrath of a Greater God*, Aaron Renfroe's eighth *Resonance Cycle* novel, puts that problem into the foreground through the offer of Ambrosia and the implications of what it removes.

That is a useful question for late-series progression fantasy. Once ordinary increases have lost some of their novelty, the nature and cost of advancement can provide tension that another impressive total cannot.

## Evaluate the trade, not just the reward

Ty's widening conflict with the Guard gives power a practical purpose, but purpose does not automatically make every means acceptable. Liberation and protection require capabilities; acquiring those capabilities can introduce a different kind of loss.

The guardian-world material also expands the operational demands. A victory has to mean more than overcoming one opponent if whole populations remain subject to the underlying arrangement.

## When the system becomes metaphysical

The trade-off for this ambition is abstraction. Readers who like clearly bounded skills may find soul-level changes and reality-shaping conflicts harder to evaluate than earlier abilities. The story needs understandable consequences to keep those developments from feeling unlimited.

The manuscript examined here is an alpha-labelled version, so line-level polish and the final edition's exact execution are outside this assessment. The structural attraction is still clear: advancement is presented as a choice with implications for identity.

Read on for that kind of high-level progression problem. Be cautious if your enjoyment depends on the rules remaining as concrete as they were near the beginning. This volume asks what an improvement is worth when the person receiving it may not emerge unchanged.
