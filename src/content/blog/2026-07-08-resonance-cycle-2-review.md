# Theater of War Review: Aaron Renfroe's Resonance Cycle Book 2 Is the Genre's Most Thoughtful Progression Fantasy

[LitRPG](blog/what-is-litrpg/) is a genre built on systems — numerical frameworks that externalize a character's growth and give readers tangible proof of progress. It is characterized by visible stat sheets, earned level-ups, and narrative tension generated by the gap between a character's current power and the challenge in front of them. What separates the genre's best work from its most formulaic is whether those systems serve story, or merely decorate it. *Theater of War*, the second book in Aaron Renfroe's *Resonance Cycle*, lands firmly in the former camp.

Based on our analysis of the opening extract and the series' structural foundation, *The Resonance Cycle* is doing something meaningfully different from most titles on the [best progression fantasy](/lists/best-progression-fantasy/) shelf right now — and it deserves your attention.

---

## What Is The Resonance Cycle Series About?

*The Resonance Cycle* follows Ty Monroe, a working-class young man from a cramped metropolitan apartment he shares with his grandmother Blaire, who has been chosen as a "scion" by a god called Inspiration to participate in something called the Resonance — a world-altering event that will eventually swap millions of people between Earth and the secondary world of Volar. Armed with a customized magical system administered by his personal Game Master (an entity called Hagemi, manifested as a crystalline D8), Ty shuttles between worlds, building power, establishing alliances, and quietly trying to protect the people he loves.

Book 2, subtitled *Theater of War*, picks up immediately after the events of Book 1. Ty returns to Earth with six months before his portal reopens, lottery monocles in his pocket, a freshly healed grandmother, and an expanding web of plans tacked to his bedroom wall.

---

## World-Building: Two Worlds, One Coherent Logic

Renfroe commits to something genuinely difficult here: a dual-world structure with a functioning time differential. Three years pass on Volar for every six months on Earth, which creates an automatic pacing pressure the author uses skillfully. Ty can't simply stay home and plan indefinitely — every Earth-side decision carries compounding weight.

What makes this feel lived-in rather than mechanical is the texture of the Earth sequences. The apartment Ty shares with Blaire is small enough that it takes a full paragraph to describe. The water filter light is still blinking. The neighbors have two families in a single-family unit. There is crime, gentrification, a recession. This isn't the standard "protagonist returns to mundane life before the adventure resumes" placeholder. It's a setting with material stakes. Ty's hundred-dollar lottery gamble with a magical monocle, and the moment he quietly leaves a scratch-off ticket with a grandmother playing her grandbabies' birthdates — that's genre craft at a level that earns comparison to the best community-rooted storytelling in [Dungeon Crawler Carl](/lists/books-like-dungeon-crawler-carl/), Matt Dinniman's benchmark for making you care about the people behind the stats.

## Progression Systems: Custom, Coherent, and Character-Driven

According to reader ratings tracked on [LitRPGTools.com](https://litrpgtools.com), titles with highly personalized progression systems — where the class and abilities feel bespoke to the specific protagonist — consistently rate higher than those using generic system templates. *The Resonance Cycle* illustrates exactly why.

Ty's class is "Merit Hunter," which allows him to custom-build abilities using a merit-point currency. His Arbiter, Hagemi, functions as a personalized Game Master — a conceit Renfroe establishes upfront is deliberate, born from his own tabletop DMing practice. What this produces narratively is a system that bends toward player ingenuity rather than grinding. The dedication — *"to the gamers who think of rules as firm suggestions, rather than hard limits"* — isn't decorative. It's a design thesis.

The opening sequence of Book 2 makes this concrete. When Ty emerges onto Earth and Hagemi externalizes his mana and abilities into a visible dome of clustered tendrils, Renfroe uses the moment to teach the reader *how the system actually works* without resorting to an infodump. Ty is learning alongside us. His realization that Wild magic suffuses all his abilities as a default — rather than being an option in his Mana Prism — is a small but precise systems beat. Dakota Krout's *Divine Dungeon* series does something similar with elemental layering; Renfroe's execution is more intimate.

Three specific system elements worth noting:

1. **The Merit-based ability customization** gives Ty (and readers) genuine agency over what the progression looks like.
2. **The mana scarcity differential between worlds** creates resource tension without artificial hand-waving — mana recharges in hours on Earth versus freely on Volar.
3. **Verdant Touch's holistic healing logic** (not just hit points but restoration of underlying damage) rewards readers who pay attention and produces one of the extract's most emotionally resonant moments.

## Character Development: Grandma Blaire Is the Secret Weapon

Here's the editorial stance: the best character writing in this extract belongs to Blaire, and Renfroe knows it.

She's introduced asleep in her clothes in front of the television — a two-sentence portrait that carries a decade of quiet sacrifice. When Ty activates Verdant Touch and restores her hearing, the payoff is immediate and completely earned: she'd lost it on the flight deck during her military service. "Sounds like damage to me," she says. That line is doing everything. It reframes the game mechanic through human experience, it establishes that Blaire is smarter than Ty gives her credit for, and it makes you want to keep reading. Will Wight builds similar emotional anchors in his secondary characters — people who make the protagonist's power feel like it has weight.

Ty himself is well-constructed: a compulsive planner, tactically minded, emotionally reserved but not emotionally absent. His discomfort with physical touch, immediately followed by his impulse to hug Blaire anyway, is the kind of behavioral nuance that distinguishes literary LitRPG from its more mechanical cousins.

## Pacing and Prose Quality

The pacing in the Book 2 opening is deliberately measured, and that's the right call. Renfroe is re-grounding readers after a Book 1 that clearly moved fast. The Earth sequences breathe. The lottery scene, Hagemi's systems tutorial, the scratch-off ticket left for a stranger, the conversation over cereal — these are paced like a writer confident enough to let small moments land before escalating.

The prose itself is clean and purposeful. Renfroe writes action-adjacent to thought in a way that keeps internal monologue from becoming static. The dedication and introduction suggest an author who knows exactly what kind of book he's writing and has chosen every element deliberately — a quality that separates *The Resonance Cycle* from the faster-produced end of the genre.

For readers tracking the [best LitRPG series](/lists/best-litrpg-books/) released in the current wave of progression fantasy, this series belongs in the same conversation as David North's *Guardian of Aster Fall* for its structural discipline, or Aaron Renfroe's own earlier work on *Spite the Dark* for its tonal confidence.

## Who This Is For

**Read *Theater of War* if you:**
- Want a dual-world LitRPG where both worlds feel consequential
- Prefer customizable, player-driven progression systems over static class trees
- Like your genre fiction to have emotional grounding alongside the stat sheets
- Enjoyed *Dungeon Crawler Carl* for its humanity, or *He Who Fights With Monsters* (Jason Cheyne) for its systematic depth
- Are looking for progression fantasy that takes its social and economic context seriously

**This may not be your entry point if:**
- You're new to LitRPG and want a simpler system — the Merit Hunter class rewards readers who enjoy engaging with mechanical complexity
- You haven't read Book 1; *Theater of War* is a true sequel and assumes familiarity
