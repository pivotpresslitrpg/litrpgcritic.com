Beta readers are test players for your book. A LitRPG novel is a system as much as a story, and no author can playtest their own system with fresh eyes — you know where the loot is buried. Someone else has to tell you the level-12 power spike breaks the tension, the crafting subsystem disappears for forty chapters, or the stat screen contradicts itself in Chapter 3.

The problem has never been convincing authors that beta readers matter. The problem is finding good ones. So let's treat this like the hiring decision it actually is.

## What Does a Beta Reader Actually Do?

A beta reader reads a complete draft and reports the reader experience back to you: where they got bored, where they got confused, where they stopped believing. They are not editors and not proofreaders. A beta's job is diagnosis, not surgery.

In LitRPG the job carries extra weight. A genre-fluent beta checks things a general-fiction reader can't:

- Does the progression math hold up, or does the protagonist's power curve skip steps?
- Are stat blocks, skills, and system rules internally consistent across 200,000 words?
- Does the build feel earned, or does it read like the author handing themselves loot?
- Do genre conventions land — or does the book accidentally rehash a mechanic every reader has seen a dozen times?

That last point is why "my cousin reads a lot" is not a beta plan. If your tester has never seen a dungeon core or a cultivation realm, their feedback on your dungeon core cultivation novel is noise with occasional signal.

## Where Do You Find LitRPG Beta Readers?

The honest answer for most of the genre's history: you begged. Discord servers, subreddit threads, newsletter swaps, and a prayer that the volunteer who ghosted your last book wouldn't ghost this one too.

There's now a more professional route. [LitRPGTools.com](https://litrpgtools.com) runs an [Industry Professionals directory](https://litrpgtools.com/industry-professionals) of vetted editors, narrators, artists, and beta readers who work in LitRPG and progression fantasy specifically. Every profile is reviewed before it goes public, and the beta reader application has a hard gate worth knowing about: applicants must confirm they've read at least 25 books in the genre before they can apply at all.

That single requirement filters out most of what makes open calls for betas so exhausting. You're browsing people who chose this genre on purpose.

The directory works like a hiring tool, not a bulletin board. You can filter by role and availability — people taking work now versus running a waitlist — and profiles list feedback types, turnaround, delivery format, and rates where the professional chooses to publish them. Betas on the platform also declare whether they'll flag stat-block and system issues and proof progression math, which is exactly the checklist above.

## How Much Do Beta Readers Cost?

Volunteer betas cost nothing and are worth every penny — sometimes brilliant, frequently late, occasionally vanished. Professional betas charge for reliability, and the pricing is more standardized than most authors expect.

Professionals on LitRPGTools set their own rates, and the platform's own application guidance tells them the industry norm: roughly **$250–500 USD per 50,000 words** for a full beta pass, with rush work typically at 1.5–2× standard. Some publish exact rates, some publish ranges, some prefer "rates on request." Against the cost of editing, cover art, and audio, a paid beta pass is one of the cheaper insurance policies in your production budget — and it's the only one that tests the actual reading experience.

Authors and publishers on the platform's paid tiers can also post a **casting call**: a private paid brief that gets matched to approved professionals whose genre comfort, availability, and rates fit the project. Instead of you searching, the qualified candidates come to you.

## How to Brief a Beta Reader So the Feedback Is Useful

A beta pass is only as good as the brief. Before anyone starts reading:

1. State genre and subgenre precisely — dungeon core, system apocalypse, cultivation, progression-heavy or story-first.
2. Give word count and your deadline honestly, including whether it's a rush job.
3. Name the two or three things you're most worried about. Betas find more when they know where to dig.
4. Agree on delivery format — inline comments, a written report, a call — and on scope, price, and turnaround in writing.
5. Ask for the bad news explicitly. A beta who only reassures you is an expensive friend.
