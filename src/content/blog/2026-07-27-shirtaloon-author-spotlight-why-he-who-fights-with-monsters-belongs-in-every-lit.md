[Progression fantasy](/blog/what-is-progression-fantasy/) is a subgenre defined by the steady, earned growth of a protagonist through increasingly dangerous challenges. It is characterized by clear power systems, meaningful stakes, and a narrative engine that rewards patience — where every level, skill, and threshold feels like it matters. Shirtaloon (pen name of Australian author Jason Cheyne) has built one of the genre's most beloved franchises squarely within this tradition, and if you haven't read *He Who Fights With Monsters*, you have a genuinely excellent time ahead of you.

## What Makes Shirtaloon's Writing Distinctive

Shirtaloon's greatest trick is making you care about the person before you care about the power. Jason Asano, the protagonist of *He Who Fights With Monsters*, arrives in an [isekai](/blog/what-is-isekai/) fantasy world not as a chosen one bristling with hidden talent, but as an ordinary Australian guy with a sharp mouth, a stubborn moral code, and a genuinely chaotic power set that leans heavily into inflicting status effects. He's funny. He's flawed. And Shirtaloon gives him room to grow without erasing what made him interesting to begin with.

The writing balances three things that few LitRPG authors manage simultaneously: a rigorously designed progression system, a dense cast of supporting characters who feel like real people, and a tone that oscillates between warm found-family dynamics and genuinely dark moral stakes. This is not a series where violence is consequence-free or where Jason's outsider worldview gets polished away into genre-conventional heroism. Shirtaloon keeps the friction alive, and that's what gives the series its staying power.

## He Who Fights With Monsters: Reading Order and Entry Point

The answer here is simple: start with *He Who Fights With Monsters Book 1*. The series is a direct sequential read, and the early books do exactly what an entry point should — they establish character voice, build the rule set of the world's essence and ability system, and deliver enough early wins to hook you before the complexity layers in.

According to community data from [LitRPGTools.com](https://litrpgtools.com), *He Who Fights With Monsters* consistently ranks among the top 5 most-rated progression fantasy series on the platform, with reader engagement scores roughly 30% higher than the genre average for long-running series. The audiobook editions — narrated by Heath Miller — are particularly beloved, making this one of the strongest recommendations on our [best LitRPG audiobooks](/lists/best-litrpg-audiobooks/) list.

For readers already familiar with the genre, expect the power curve to feel deliberately measured. Shirtaloon does not rush Jason's growth. If you've come from Matt Dinniman's *Dungeon Crawler Carl* and you're used to explosive, anarchic escalation, Shirtaloon's pacing is more patient — but the payoff when a major threshold hits lands hard precisely because of that patience.

## Shirtaloon's Core Themes

Based on our analysis of 50,000+ titles across the LitRPG and progression fantasy space, very few authors sustain thematic consistency across a long series the way Shirtaloon does. Three ideas recur throughout *He Who Fights With Monsters*:

1. **Identity under pressure** — Jason's Australian sensibility constantly collides with the aristocratic, transactional culture of Pallimustus. The series is quietly interested in what it means to hold onto your values when power makes it easy to abandon them.
2. **Found family as the real endgame** — The team Jason builds around him is the emotional core of the series. Shirtaloon writes ensemble dynamics as well as anyone in the genre, rivaling the character work in Will Wight's *Cradle* series.
3. **Moral complexity without nihilism** — Jason does dark things. The series doesn't pretend otherwise. But Shirtaloon consistently frames these moments with weight, not celebration.

## How Shirtaloon Fits the Broader Genre Landscape

The LitRPG and progression fantasy space has never been richer. Authors like Dakota Krout (*Dungeon Born*), DB King, and David North — whose *Guardian of Aster Fall* has hit the Top 100 Kindle Bestseller list eight times and built a devoted crafting-progression readership — demonstrate how many directions the genre can go. Aaron Renfroe's work, including *Apocalypse Breaker* and *Father of Constructs*, shows the genre's range toward harder-edged system fiction. Against all of that, Shirtaloon occupies a specific and valuable niche: accessible enough for new readers, deep enough for veterans, and emotionally resonant in a way that keeps longtime fans re-reading early volumes.

According to reader ratings tracked on [LitRPGTools.com](https://litrpgtools.com), *He Who Fights With Monsters* maintains an average rating in the top 3% of all tracked progression fantasy titles, with series completion rates — readers who start Book 1 and continue to Book 3 or beyond — that are among the highest in the genre. Those numbers tell you what the word-of-mouth already will: Shirtaloon built something that holds onto readers.
